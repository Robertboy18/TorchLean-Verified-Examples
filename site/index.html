Robert Joseph George *

Here's my vision for verification in AI. Some examples I've been working on :)

* I wrote most of these posts, with some AI assistance in the prose and Lean code.

01 / Inference

Thinking Machines Lab’s batch-invariance problem, formalized

The same prompt can get a different answer when the batch changes, even with sampling turned off! I wanted to understand why. So we start with three numbers, follow the rounding through the calculation, and prove when a change in the scores still leaves the same winning token.

02 / Circuit verification

Checking a small transformer in Lean

Neel Somani checks a small transformer with circuits and an SMT solver. I trained his model with his own code and brought the checkpoint into Lean, so we can check its exported decisions on all 256 task inputs and then recompute them from the weights.

03 / Training

Training a GPT-2-style model in Lean

Let's train a GPT in Lean :) I trained a 124.4-million-parameter model on one A100 and compared fifty matched updates with PyTorch. The loss went down, but getting a dependable assistant out of it was harder.

Along the way we prove results about next-token targets, resuming training, and reusing the attention cache during generation.

04 / Tensor programming

Writing tensor programs in Lean

I'd like to write a tensor calculation in Lean and choose the device when I run it. So we try the custom-function API, build attention with einsum, and look at the measured performance. Higher derivatives and binary128 arithmetic let us try other calculations too.

05 / Kimi K3

Kimi K3: the paper and proofs side by side

I wanted to bring the paper-and-proof approach from research mathematics to ML. With K3's report open beside us, we work through its memory, attention, and experts and prove that streaming preserves the logits. We also find a slip at one endpoint of the expert-balancing argument.

06 / Nemotron

Nemotron 3 Ultra: replaying a rounded state

After K3, I wanted to look at NVIDIA's Nemotron and its recurrent memory, which could save its state less often by recomputing recent inputs. We prove when that replay preserves the state, and then check a case where fewer rounded writes leave a larger final error.
